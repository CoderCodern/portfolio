I first considered pursuing a management role when I joined a large corporation. I observed managers who spoke with confidence and maintained control over their teams. It seemed impressive, and I thought to myself, "I want to become an Engineering Manager!" However, I quickly realized that I had little understanding of what that truly entailed.

Fast forward two years, and I discovered an important truth: the journey to engineering leadership does not begin with an immediate leap into management. Instead, it starts with mastering both technical and soft skills.

The key insight I gained was that before stepping into an Engineering Leader role, one must first become a senior developer. This entails being qualified and having a proven track record. I would like to share the lessons I learned during this time and how you can advance if you aspire to become an engineering leader.

## Types of Engineering Leaders

Most engineering leadership roles can be categorized into two main paths: the Individual Contributor (IC) path and the management path.

### The IC Path: Staff Engineers

Staff Engineers are akin to senior developers, but their influence is broader. They may focus on a single team or span multiple teams, depending on their assignments.

A typical organizational structure might look like this:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fe81b9304-dcee-4a3d-ae4a-febaa076f0cd_1340x708.png)

Senior developers primarily concentrate on their specific team, with one designated as the technical leader. Above them are the Staff Engineers.

The daily responsibilities of Staff Engineers encompass:

- Technical strategy
- Technical innovation
- Problem-solving
- Cross-team collaboration

The key distinction between Staff Engineers and Engineering Managers is that Staff Engineers do not oversee people; their focus is strictly on technical matters. For instance, if an organization plans to transition from a monolithic application to microservices, Staff Engineers will strategize the necessary steps. They will determine how to segment the domains, migrate applications without downtime, and develop testing plans. Collaboration with Engineering Managers and senior developers is essential to address these challenges.

As they continue to make significant contributions, Staff Engineers may advance to principal engineer roles and beyond.

### The Management Path: Engineering Managers

The management path includes roles such as Engineering Managers (EMs), Directors, and Vice Presidents of Engineering. These individuals are responsible for team and people management.

Typically, they oversee entire teams, which encompass the three pillars of team dynamics:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2Fbcfff085-e74c-4ceb-9550-616f9c9dd192_1166x1076.png)

Role definitions can vary significantly across organizations. For example, some EMs may engage directly in project management, while others work alongside Product Managers (PMs) for support. Their primary responsibility is to ensure the team progresses effectively.

One certainty is that EMs are tasked with people management.

## Why Becoming a Senior Developer is the First Step to Leadership

To understand this, we must first discuss the engineering ladder. Engineering ladders serve as a framework for companies to define their engineer levels. Each organization may have its own ladder or adopt one from well-established companies. Resources like [levels.fyi](https://www.levels.fyi/) provide examples.

The primary measure of levels is based on impact, typically evaluated as follows:

- **Level 1**: Impact achieved through task completion
- **Level 2**: Impact achieved by leading projects
- **Level 3**: Impact achieved by leading a single team
- **Level 4**: Impact achieved by leading multiple teams (or a large, complex team)
- **Level 5**: Impact achieved by leading an entire division

This framework applies to both IC and management paths. A visual representation is as follows:

![](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F5d08bf95-b550-48cf-b9b5-f67b49fb37c3_1406x928.png)

It is evident that leadership roles, whether on the IC or management path, require individuals to be at least at the third level. The expectation is that individuals in these roles will lead an entire team.

Thus, becoming a senior developer is your initial step toward any engineering leadership position.

## Embracing the Senior Developer Mindset

As previously mentioned, a senior developer functions as a team leader, a role that propels the entire team forward.

If you are uncertain about your current position or grade, consider this question: "Are you capable of making most technical decisions for your team?" If your answer is yes, congratulations—you are likely a senior developer.

If you are unsure, don't worry. Here are some traits commonly associated with senior developers to help you identify any core skills you may still need to develop.

**1. Team Ownership**

A senior developer prioritizes the team over individual contributions. When team members encounter challenges, they should be attentive and proactive. While you don't need to handle every task personally, you must stay informed about the team's status, including the roadmap, outstanding issues, and significant technical debts.

This also involves actively identifying and addressing issues without waiting for direction. Senior developers lead by providing guidance rather than waiting for it.

**2. Vision & Strategy**

Senior developers adopt a long-term perspective rather than focusing solely on immediate successes. They envision what the system should look like in 2-3 years and develop strategies to realize that vision.

For instance, they may aim to delegate development responsibilities to other teams as the current service evolves into a Backend for Frontend (BFF) service. They will work to enhance maintainability through code modularization, ensuring adequate test coverage, and creating comprehensive documentation and processes. This may take 1-2 years to achieve, but senior developers will strategically plan these tasks in each planning session.

**3. Go Beyond Engineering**

Senior developers should engage with non-engineering stakeholders, such as product managers, designers, and quality assurance professionals. A broader understanding of product delivery enhances their ability to troubleshoot issues effectively.

They also maintain regular communication with business stakeholders to ensure they ask the right questions when necessary. For example, if the business side presents an unusual requirement, senior developers will inquire about the rationale and seek better alternatives to address the problem.

## Timeline

In the current job market, senior developers typically require 6-8+ years of experience. However, this can vary significantly among individuals. I've seen some achieve senior status in just 1-2 years, while others with 15 years of experience may still be performing at a mid-level. There is no right or wrong here; it is perfectly acceptable to be content with your current position.

As long as you maintain the right mindset and continually develop your skills, the timeline becomes less significant. Keep progressing and learning consistently, and you will reach your goals in due time.
