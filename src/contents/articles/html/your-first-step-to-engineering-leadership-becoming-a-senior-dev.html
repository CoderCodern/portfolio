Your Path to Engineering Leadership: Senior Dev Comes First

April 2025 6 min read Career · Leadership · Engineering

A guest post from Adler Hsieh — author of Tokyo Tech Lead, a newsletter on engineering leadership and the transition from software engineer to engineering leader. His central point: the path to leading engineers doesn't start with wanting a management title. It starts with becoming senior.

Adler first wanted to be a manager after joining a large corporation. He watched managers speak with confidence, maintain control over their teams, and thought — "I want that." He had almost no idea what it actually entailed.

Two years later, he found the key insight that reframed everything: the journey to engineering leadership doesn't begin with a leap into management. It begins with mastering the technical and soft skills that make you worth following. Before you can lead engineers, you need to be a senior engineer.

"I want to become an Engineering Manager!" is a perfectly valid ambition. "I understand what that means" — that part takes longer.

the two paths

Types of Engineering Leaders

Engineering leadership splits into two main paths: the Individual Contributor (IC) path and the management path. Both require leadership. Neither is the easy option.

👥 Management path — people focus
Engineering Manager, Director, VP of Engineering
Responsible for team performance and career growth
Primary output: a team that delivers well
🔧 IC path — technical focus
Staff Engineer, Principal Engineer, Distinguished Engineer
Responsible for technical strategy and cross-team direction
Primary output: systems and decisions that scale

The IC Path: Staff Engineers

Staff Engineers are senior developers with broader reach. They may own a single team's technical direction or span multiple teams, depending on the organization. Their daily work covers technical strategy, cross-team problem-solving, and technical innovation. The key distinction: Staff Engineers don't manage people. Their influence comes entirely through technical credibility and judgment.

From Staff Engineer, the ladder continues up to Principal, Distinguished, and Fellow levels — each with wider scope and higher stakes.

The Management Path: Engineering Managers

Engineering Managers (EMs), Directors, and VPs of Engineering own team and people management. Role definitions vary — some EMs do project management directly, others work alongside PMs. But people management is always the core. EMs are accountable for the team's progress, health, and growth — not just the code that ships.

Both paths eventually manage something. Staff Engineers manage complexity. Engineering Managers manage humans. Pick the one that sounds less exhausting to you.

why senior first

Why Becoming a Senior Developer is the First Step

The answer lives in the engineering ladder — the framework most companies use to define engineer levels. The primary measure at each level is impact:

  • Level 1 — impact through completing assigned tasks
  • Level 2 — impact through leading projects
  • Level 3 — impact through leading a single team
  • Level 4 — impact through leading multiple teams
  • Level 5 — impact through leading an entire division

Leadership roles — on both the IC and management path — start at Level 3. That means your baseline expectation is leading an entire team. That's what senior developers do. You can't skip that level and walk into a leadership role cold. The ladder doesn't bend.

"The journey to engineering leadership doesn't begin with a leap into management. It starts with the skills and track record that make you worth following."

So "I want to be an engineering leader" is a goal. "I need to become a senior dev first" is the plan. Those are different things, and conflating them is the most common mistake Adler saw at large organizations.

the mindset

Embracing the Senior Developer Mindset

A senior developer functions as a team leader — someone who moves the whole team forward, not just their own tickets. Here's a quick self-check: can you make most technical decisions for your team without escalating to someone else? If yes, you're probably already there. If not, that gap is your roadmap.

Three traits consistently define senior developers:

Team Ownership

Senior devs prioritize the team over their own output. When teammates hit blockers, they stay aware and proactive — not to solve every problem personally, but to know the state of the road: the roadmap, the outstanding issues, the technical debt that actually matters. They lead by providing guidance rather than waiting for it.

Vision and Strategy

Senior devs think in 2-3 year arcs, not sprint cycles. They envision what the system should look like and build toward it deliberately — modularizing code, ensuring test coverage, writing documentation that the next team can actually use. It might take 18 months of incremental planning sessions. They plan for it anyway.

Going Beyond Engineering

Senior devs engage with non-engineering stakeholders: product managers, designers, QA. A wider view of product delivery makes them better at catching where things go wrong early — and at asking the right questions before the wrong thing gets built. When a business requirement looks strange, they ask why instead of just implementing it.

🔵 Mid-level habits
Completes assigned work well
Asks for technical direction when stuck
Focused on own output and PR queue
🟡 Senior habits
Owns the team's technical direction
Provides guidance without waiting to be asked
Monitors overall team progress, blockers, and health
💡 Tip

Three resources Adler recommends for the full senior engineer picture: The Software Engineer's Guidebook by Gergely Orosz, roadmap.sh for skill maps, and Engineering Ladders for level frameworks.

✓ Verdict

Team Ownership, Vision and Strategy, and cross-functional engagement — these three traits separate senior developers from mid-level developers. All of them are learnable. None of them require a title first.

timeline

Timeline

In the current job market, senior developers typically have 6-8+ years of experience. That's a median. Don't mistake it for a rule.

⚠️ Warning

Adler has seen engineers reach senior in 1-2 years. He's also seen 15-year veterans still operating at mid-level. Years of experience measures time, not growth. The timeline is a side effect of mindset and deliberate skill-building — not the other way around.

There's no shame in wherever you are right now. Senior isn't a deadline — it's a capability threshold. Keep building the skills that compound, stay curious about the work beyond your own tasks, and the level follows naturally.

"As long as you maintain the right mindset and consistently develop your skills, the timeline becomes irrelevant. Keep progressing, keep learning, and you'll get there."
