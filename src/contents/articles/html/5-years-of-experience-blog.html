5 Years of Experience OR 5 Times 1 Year of Experience?

March 2025 5 min read Career · Seniority · Growth

I remember someone telling me after an interview that the candidate we just saw did not have 5 years of experience — but 5 times 1 year of experience instead. It was a devastating diagnosis. And the more I thought about it, the more I realised how easy it is to fall into that trap.

This person kept working on small, very straightforward projects in similar phases. Because of this, they never really developed their skills — and most of their years on paper didn't matter compared to candidates who had been exposed to a broader range of problems and challenges.

Of course, it makes sense that years in the industry don't directly translate to seniority. But surprisingly, it's often assumed that people will steadily get better over time. The trick is that it's not steady, and there is a ceiling to what you can accomplish if you stay at the same job and never leave your comfort zone.

Years of experience is not the same thing as experience. One is a timestamp. The other is a scar.

not all roles

Not All Roles Are Equal

Not all jobs or projects give you the same opportunity for growth. If you're stuck in a position where all you're asked to do is simple frontend work on landing pages with no architectural decisions and no senior colleagues — there's only so far you can go.

Every job has a skill ceiling. The question is: how high is it? Basically, how much can you actually learn if you keep doing this for years?

The Skill Ceiling

I like the idea of a "skill ceiling" — often used in video games, defined as the amount of skill required to master some activity. Here's what it looks like for a typical role:

You'll learn fast at first, reaching the maximum skill level for that position. You might become very good at that specific thing — but anyone could reach your level in a couple of years. If you keep going past that point:

  • At best, you'll improve marginally.
  • At worst, you'll stagnate — or develop bad habits and actually regress.

Raising the Skill Ceiling

If you recognise that you've stopped learning new things, you need to address it. While you stay in this particular role, you'll probably be fine. But if you ever have to switch, you'll be labelled as someone with x times 1 year of experience — simultaneously over-qualified on paper and under-qualified in reality.

Your company's role here matters. Part of a manager's job is to provide growth opportunities and visibility over a career path. Colleagues can also help — through inspiration, mentorship, or just raising the bar of what "good" looks like in the room.

⚠️ Red Flag

If your current company is actively keeping you out of growth opportunities, that's a serious warning sign. They may be creating dependency: you lose external options, lose negotiating power, and become tied to that single job's health and goodwill. That's a trap, not a career.

comfort zone

The Comfort Zone

You can hit a skill plateau even in a role with a high ceiling — if you're not actively seeking growth. After a few years in a position, you know the job. You can meet expectations without much effort. People appreciate your work. It doesn't require much energy anymore.

The comfort zone is called that for a reason. It's genuinely comfortable. That's the whole problem.

The Comfort Zone Is a Long-Term Risk

While it's pleasant short-term, the long-term result is the same as a low-ceiling job: you end up repeating the same year of experience, staying locked in your current position while the world changes around you.

Things move fast. You don't want to find yourself dependent on a single company's goodwill and health. Doing the same familiar thing for years can also become quietly demoralising — tasks that were once engaging can turn dreadful when stripped of novelty.

Breaking Out Requires Effort

Cal Newport's book So Good They Can't Ignore You frames this well. Think of skills and knowledge as career capital — currency you can spend to get the job and life you want. But not all skills are equal:

"Basic economic theory tells us that if you want something that's both rare and valuable, you need something rare and valuable to offer in return — this is Supply and Demand 101. If you want a great job, you need something of great value to offer in return."

— Cal Newport, So Good They Can't Ignore You

Geoff Colvin captures why most people don't do this:

"Doing things we know how to do well is enjoyable, and that's exactly the opposite of what deliberate practice demands. Deliberate practice is above all an effort of focus and concentration. That is what makes it 'deliberate', as distinct from the mindless playing of scales that most people engage in."

— Geoff Colvin, Talent Is Overrated

Newport builds on this directly:

"If you show up and do what you're told, you will reach an 'acceptable level' of ability before plateauing. The good news about deliberate practice is that it will push you past this plateau and into a realm where you have little competition. The bad news is that the reason so few people accomplish this feat is exactly because deliberate practice is often the opposite of enjoyable."

— Cal Newport, referencing Colvin

sustainable growth

Sustainable Skill and Career Growth

Growth Is Not Linear

Two things worth internalising before you spiral into a growth obsession:

  • Reaching a plateau is normal. Maybe business constraints require steady execution for a while. Maybe you've hit your mental capacity and need some time on straightforward tasks to recharge. Plateaus happen. They're fine.
  • Wanting to stay in your comfort zone is normal. You might be tired, dealing with health, family, or just needing to breathe. Choosing short-term comfort knowingly is not a failure — it's a tradeoff. Make it consciously and you retain control.

Trying to be in permanent high-growth mode is not realistic across a whole career. Life is long: you'll have kids, fall sick, move cities, break a leg, focus on a hobby. Unless your job is your single priority, there will be periods of limited professional growth — and that's completely fine.

💡 The Key Insight

The difference between choosing a comfort period and drifting into one is awareness. One is strategic rest. The other is slow erosion you don't notice until the options have already closed.

A Realistic Approach

Staying in the comfort zone for too long is the real risk. The sustainable answer is to alternate: periods of plateau (recovery, focus, life events) and periods of deliberate growth (new role, new responsibilities, new challenges).

❌ The trap
Same projects, same stack, same team for years
Avoid feedback or challenges that feel risky
Optimise for comfort over stretch
Assume more years = more value automatically
✅ The way out
Deliberately seek roles or tasks that feel hard
Treat plateaus as planned recovery, not default mode
Build career capital in rare, high-value skills
Stay aware of your own ceiling — and raise it intentionally

In this chart we see someone getting good at a job, plateauing, gaining new responsibilities and growing again — then deliberately stepping back for a personal event, then refocusing. That's not a failure arc. That's a life lived with some intention.

✓ Verdict

The goal isn't constant growth. It's intentional growth — knowing when you're choosing a plateau versus drifting into one. Build rare skills. Stay aware of your ceiling. Give yourself permission to rest, as long as you're the one choosing to.
