How Coding Saved Me from Myself

December 2024 4 min read Career · Personal · Mental Health

Have you ever felt like your own thoughts were out to get you? Like no matter what you do, your brain just won't stop running in circles? That was me for the longest time. I'd overthink everything — lying in bed, walking around, just trying to relax. My mind always found something to obsess over. It was exhausting.

And then coding came into my life. Looking back, it almost feels like a natural progression. Ever since I was a kid, I loved tinkering with computers — figuring out how they worked, hardware and software both. That curiosity always kept me wondering what else I could learn or create.

At first, it was just something random I decided to try. I figured: "Hey, this could be fun, maybe even useful." But it turned out to be so much more. Coding didn't just fill my time — it gave me something to focus on, something that actually helped me deal with all the noise in my head.

a distraction

A Fun Distraction

When you're coding, you can't zone out or let your mind wander. You've got to focus — like, really focus. Writing code, fixing bugs, or figuring out why your app isn't working demands all your attention. For me, that was exactly what I needed. It was like giving my brain a puzzle to solve instead of letting it spiral into overthinking.

"It wasn't just a distraction — it felt like I was doing something meaningful. That combination is harder to find than it sounds."

Turns out the cure for too many thoughts is one very specific, very annoying thought: why is this not working.

overthinking

Turning Overthinking into Problem-Solving

Overthinking is basically your brain trying to solve problems that don't exist. Coding took that same restless energy and pointed it at actual problems. Instead of stressing about things I couldn't control, I was debugging, building, and figuring out how to make things work. Channeling that anxiety into something real and fixable was a game-changer.

🧠 Overthinking mode
Solving problems that don't exist
Analyzing texts for hidden meaning
Staying up replaying conversations
💻 Problem-solving mode
Debugging actual broken code
Figuring out why the layout is wrong
Staying up because the fix is almost there
creativity

Creativity Meets Logic

I'm into front-end development, and what I love most about it is that it isn't purely logical — it's creative. You're designing how things look and feel, taking ideas in your head and making them real on a screen. It's art meets problem-solving, and that combination never gets old.

Front-end dev: where you spend three hours making a button feel right and consider it a productive afternoon. And it genuinely is.

💡 Tip

If you lean creative but feel intimidated by "real programming," start with HTML and CSS. The feedback loop is instant and visual — you see results before you've written a single function.

the escape

Escaping the Overthinking Trap

When I was in a relationship, my overthinking hit a whole new level — analyzing every text, every conversation, every silence. Coding became my escape. Tech is always changing. There's always something new to learn. Instead of spiraling, I'd dive into the latest framework, a new CSS feature, a design pattern I hadn't tried. It pulled me out of my head and into something genuinely engaging.

"Instead of spiraling about things I couldn't control, I'd lose myself in something I could build. That trade is almost always worth it."

little wins

The Little Wins

You know that feeling when you finally fix a bug or your code works exactly as intended? It's disproportionately satisfying. Even something small — getting a button to look just right, a layout snapping into place — felt like a win. Those micro-victories kept me going and gave me a sense of momentum that carried into the rest of my life.

The bug you've been staring at for two hours disappears the moment you explain it to someone else. Rubber duck debugging is not a joke. It works.

mistakes

Learning to Embrace Mistakes

Coding rewired how I think about failure. Every error message, every broken feature, every wrong assumption was just part of the process — not a verdict on my ability. It made me genuinely okay with not getting things right the first time, as long as I kept iterating. That lesson migrated out of the editor and into the rest of my life.

✓ Verdict

Errors are information, not judgments. The best coders aren't the ones who write perfect code — they're the ones who read error messages carefully and know what to try next. That's a skill, and you can build it.

building

Building Something from Scratch

There's something quietly powerful about starting with a blank screen and ending with something real. Coding gave me the ability to create tools, apps, and designs from nothing — and pointing to something and saying "I made that" carries more weight than I expected. It's a consistent reminder that you have agency, even when everything else feels out of your control.

Starting a new project feels like unlimited potential. Finishing one feels like a small superpower. The messy middle is where most people stop — don't be most people.

community

A Community That Gets It

One thing I didn't expect: the community. Forums, meetups, tech creators, open-source projects — there are a lot of people who share this passion, and being around them is genuinely energizing. You learn from others, occasionally teach what you know, and realize you're part of something that's always moving forward.

💡 Tip

You don't have to be an expert to participate. Ask questions, share what you're building, help someone who's a step behind where you are. The community compounds your learning faster than solo study alone.

wrapping up

Wrapping It Up

Coding didn't just distract me from overthinking — it gave me a different way to approach life entirely. It taught me to focus, to be patient, to treat mistakes as data, and to find satisfaction in making things. And honestly, it's just fun.

If you're feeling stuck in your own head, give it a shot. You don't need to become an expert or make it your career. Just try building something — anything. You might find, like I did, that the act of creation is exactly what quiets the noise.

"Coding didn't just fill my time. It gave me something to focus on — and that turned out to be exactly what I needed."
